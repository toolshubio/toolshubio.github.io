What Is an External Link Counter?

Every link on your page pointing to a different domain is an external (outbound) link. Search engines treat them as votes of confidence — and as potential liabilities. A page leaking dozens of outbound links dilutes the authority it passes internally, and links to dead or shady destinations actively hurt trust. Counting them is the first step to managing them.

The counter fetches a page's HTML, extracts every <a> tag, and classifies each link: internal (same domain) versus external (different domain), dofollow versus nofollow, unique destinations versus repeats. The breakdown matters more than the total — ten links to one trusted source is a different story than ten links to ten random sites.

Why count instead of just eyeballing? Because modern pages hide links everywhere: navigation menus, footers, sidebar widgets, author bios, embedded scripts. A typical blog post shows 5 visible content links and carries 60+ total once templates are included. You cannot audit what you cannot see.

How to Use the External Link Counter

Paste the page URL into the tool and run the scan. Review the totals: external count, internal count, dofollow/nofollow split, and the list of unique external domains. Click through the external destinations — or better, run them against a broken-link checker — and decide what stays.

Healthy patterns to aim for: outbound links that are editorial (you chose them because they help the reader), a nofollow attribute on sponsored, affiliate, or user-generated links, and zero links to dead pages. Red flags: dozens of footer links to unrelated sites (a classic hacked-site symptom), affiliate links missing nofollow or sponsored attributes, and external links on pages that should be tightly focused, like checkout or signup flows.

Re-run the counter quarterly on important pages. Link rot is constant — studies consistently find a meaningful share of web links die within a few years — and a page you audited in January can be linking to a domain parker by October.

How External Link Counting Works

The scan is a small, fast crawl of a single page.

Step 1 — Page fetch. The tool requests the URL like a browser and retrieves the HTML source. JavaScript-rendered links may be missed by simple fetchers; better tools render the page fully.

Step 2 — Link extraction. Every anchor tag's href is collected, along with its rel attribute (nofollow, sponsored, ugc) and anchor text.

Step 3 — Classification. Each URL's domain is compared against the page's domain. Same domain (including subdomains, depending on settings) counts as internal; everything else is external. Relative URLs resolve against the page address first.

Step 4 — Deduplication and reporting. Totals are computed two ways: all link instances (showing template repetition) and unique destinations (showing real outbound breadth). The report lists both.

Dofollow/nofollow classification reads the rel attribute directly — nofollow, sponsored, and ugc all tell search engines not to pass ranking credit, which is exactly what you want on paid or untrusted links.

Frequently asked

Is this external link counter free?

Yes — single-page scans are free with no account required.

How many external links should a page have?

There is no magic number. Relevance beats count — a 3,000-word guide with 15 good outbound links is healthier than a 300-word page with 15 random ones.

Do external links hurt my SEO?

No — linking to quality sources is normal and healthy. Problems come from excessive, irrelevant, or broken outbound links, and from paid links missing proper attributes.

What's the difference between nofollow and sponsored?

Both withhold ranking credit. Use sponsored for paid/affiliate links and nofollow for untrusted or general cases; ugc marks user-generated content.

Can it find broken external links?

Counters list destinations; pair the output with a broken-link checker (or a tool with status-code testing) to find dead ones.

Does it count links in navigation and footers?

Yes — the scan reads the full page HTML, including template links most people forget exist.

Can I check a competitor's page?

Yes. Any public URL can be scanned — it is a standard part of competitive link analysis.
