Early in my SEO days I "optimized" a page by repeating the target keyword 47 times in 800 words. It read like a robot wrote it — because, effectively, one had. A keyword density checker is the smoke alarm for that mistake: paste your draft, and it shows each word and phrase's frequency as a percentage, so you can see at a glance whether your writing sounds natural or stuffed.

What Is Keyword Density?

Keyword density is simple arithmetic: (times a keyword appears ÷ total words) × 100. A keyword appearing 5 times in 100 words has 5% density. The metric dates from an era when search engines counted repetitions crudely — modern Google does not use density as a ranking factor and publishes no ideal percentage.

So why check it at all? As a sanity signal. Writing naturally about a topic almost always lands your main terms between roughly 0.5% and 2.5% without trying. When the checker shows one phrase towering at 8% while everything else sits under 1%, that is not optimization — it is a readability problem wearing an SEO costume. The number is the symptom; awkward prose is the disease.

Good checkers analyze more than single words. Two-word and three-word phrases (bigrams and trigrams) reveal the long-tail patterns single-word counts miss — "coffee maker" behaving very differently from "coffee" and "maker" separately. That phrase-level view is where the real insights hide.

How to Use the Keyword Density Checker

Paste your draft text — or enter a live URL on tools that support it — and run the analysis. Review the top of the frequency table: your main topic terms should appear prominently but not dominantly. Anything above ~3% deserves a read-aloud test; anything important sitting near 0% may signal a topical gap.

Work the results in this order: first fix stuffing (rewrite repeated phrases with synonyms and pronouns — your readers will thank you), then check coverage (are the subtopics your competitors cover represented in your phrase list?), then re-run to confirm the table looks balanced. One pass is usually enough; this is a smoke alarm, not a dashboard to watch daily.

Toggle the stop-word filter on. Words like "the," "and," and "is" will otherwise dominate every table and bury the meaningful terms. Filtering them surfaces the topical vocabulary you actually came to inspect.

How Keyword Density Analysis Works

The analysis is straightforward text processing.

Step 1 — Tokenization. Your text is split into words (lowercased, punctuation stripped) and the total word count is established as the denominator.

Step 2 — Stop-word filtering. Common function words are optionally removed so the frequency table reflects topical terms rather than grammar.

Step 3 — N-gram counting. Single words, two-word phrases, and three-word phrases are each counted and ranked. Density is computed per phrase as count ÷ total words × 100.

Step 4 — Reporting. Results sort by frequency with density percentages, often with visual bars. Some tools flag phrases crossing warning thresholds or compare against a target keyword you specify.

URL-based checkers add a fetch step: they pull the page's HTML and analyze only the body content, excluding navigation, footers, and markup — mirroring roughly what search engines evaluate.

Frequently asked

Is this keyword density checker free?

Yes — free with no account required.

What is a good keyword density?

Google publishes no ideal number and does not use density as a ranking factor. Treat 1–3% as a sanity-check zone, not a target — natural writing lands there on its own.

Can high keyword density get me penalized?

No fixed threshold triggers a penalty. Google judges keyword stuffing by how the page reads — if it sounds manipulated to a human, it is a problem regardless of the percentage.

Should I check single words or phrases?

Both. Single words show topical focus; 2- and 3-word phrases reveal the long-tail patterns that actually match search queries.

Why filter out stop words?

Words like "the" and "and" appear constantly in every text. Filtering them lets the frequency table show the topical vocabulary you are actually analyzing.

Can I check a competitor's page?

Yes — URL-based checkers analyze any public page, making density comparison part of standard competitive research.

How often should I check density?

Once per draft, before publishing. It is a pre-flight check, not an ongoing metric to optimize.
