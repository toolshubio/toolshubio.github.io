What Is an Image Merger?

An image merger (also called an image combiner) joins multiple pictures into a single image file. You choose how they sit together — side by side, stacked vertically, or in a grid — and the tool handles alignment, spacing, and the background between them.

The scenarios are everywhere once you start noticing them. A realtor posts a before/after renovation pair as one image. A support agent stacks three screenshots into a single tutorial visual instead of attaching five files. A seller shows four product angles in one listing photo. A platform that accepts only one image per post (LinkedIn cards, some CMS editors, Twitter cards) practically demands this tool.

What I appreciate about the browser-based mergers: nothing uploads. The combination runs on the Canvas API in your own browser, so private photos — client work, documents, family — never touch a server. The result downloads as a clean PNG or JPG with no tool watermark on it.

How to Merge Images Online

Step 1 — Upload your images. Add at least two pictures to the upload area. JPG, PNG, GIF, and WebP all work in the same merge, and there is no practical cap on how many you can add.

Step 2 — Pick a layout. Horizontal places images left to right (wide result); vertical stacks them top to bottom (tall result); grid arranges them in rows and columns. Drag thumbnails to reorder — for a before/after, "before" goes left or on top, always.

Step 3 — Tune spacing and background. Set a gap between images (20–40 px is the clean default; 0–100 px is typical range), pick a background color, and optionally add borders or rounded corners. White is cleanest for comparisons; brand colors work for marketing; transparent keeps a PNG flexible.

Step 4 — Merge and download. Export as PNG (lossless, keeps transparency), JPEG (smaller files, quality slider 60–100%), or WebP (best of both for web use).

Habit worth building: merge at the resolution you need before combining. Upscaling tiny originals into a big merge just makes the softness bigger — resize thoughtfully first.

Mixed sizes are handled gracefully: in horizontal mode images scale proportionally to a matching height; in vertical mode to a matching width; grid cells fit each image uniformly. Nothing gets cropped unless you explicitly choose the crop-to-match sizing option. The match-sizes control typically offers keep-original, match-largest, match-smallest, or crop-to-match — pick match-largest when you want the biggest possible shared frame.

Practical Tips for Clean Merges

  • Match orientations deliberately. Two portraits side by side work; a portrait and a landscape in one row create awkward dead space — consider a grid instead.
  • Use a real gap. 20–40 px of background between images reads as intentional; 0 px looks like a glitch, 100 px looks disconnected.
  • Keep backgrounds consistent. One shared background color unifies the merge; mismatched photo edges make it look assembled.
  • Order matters. Drag to sequence: before→after, step 1→step 2, problem→solution. The eye reads left-to-right, top-to-bottom.
  • Export PNG for graphics. Screenshots and text-heavy merges stay crisp as PNG; photos are fine as high-quality JPEG.
  • Watch the total size. Four 4000px photos merged make a giant file — resize or compress originals first if the merge is for the web.
  • Add text after merging. Labels like "Before"/"After" belong on the final combined image, positioned once rather than per photo.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this image merger free?

Yes — free, no account needed, and it does not stamp any watermark on your exported image.

How many images can I merge at once?

There is no small cap. The practical limit is your browser's ability to draw the combined result — extremely large merges may be scaled down, and the tool shows the final size.

Can I merge images of different sizes?

Yes. JPG, PNG, GIF, and WebP mix freely. Images scale proportionally to match (height for horizontal, width for vertical) or you can use the match-sizes control to keep, match, or crop to uniform sizes.

What is the difference between horizontal and vertical merging?

Horizontal places images left to right for a wide result (ideal for comparisons); vertical stacks them top to bottom for a tall result (ideal for sequences and tutorials).

Can I add spacing and a background color?

Yes. Spacing typically runs 0–100 px, with optional border width, rounded corners, and any background color — or transparent for PNG output.

Which format should I download?

PNG for lossless quality and transparency, JPEG with 80–100% quality for small photo files, WebP for web use with the best size-to-quality balance.

Are my images uploaded to a server?

No. The merge runs entirely in your browser via the Canvas API; your files are never uploaded.

Can I merge screenshots for a tutorial?

That is one of the best uses. Stack screenshots vertically, add a small gap, export as PNG for crisp text, and you have a single tutorial visual instead of scattered attachments.
