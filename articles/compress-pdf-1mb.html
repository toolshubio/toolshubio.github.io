Why 1MB Is the Line Most Portals Draw

One megabyte is the most common file-size ceiling I run into, and there is a reason. For portal operators, 1 MB per document is the compromise between legibility and server load: small enough that thousands of simultaneous uploads do not choke their systems, large enough that a multi-page document still reads clearly. Adobe's own guidance on PDF compression names upload limits as the first reason people compress — job applications, bids, tenders, pitches, and government forms routinely refuse anything heavier.

Email pushes you the same way. Gmail and Outlook cap attachments at about 25 MB total, which sounds generous until you attach four statements, a contract, and a tax return — then you are suddenly over, and re-exporting each file one by one is a chore nobody has time for.

There is also a less obvious reason to target 1 MB: everything gets faster. Smaller PDFs open quicker, download quicker, and render quicker in browser viewers. When a bank officer or hiring manager opens your file on a phone, a 1 MB document appears almost instantly while the 15 MB original stutters through its pages.

How Target-Size Compression Reaches 1MB

Getting a 10 MB PDF to 1 MB sounds aggressive, but for the documents people usually submit, it is routine. The math works because scanned pages are wildly inefficient: a phone photo of a page at full resolution can be 3 MB, while the same page at 150 DPI — still perfectly readable — is 150 KB. Twenty such pages drop from 60 MB to 3 MB on resolution alone, and quality tuning takes it the rest of the way.

Step 1 — Downsample. Images are reduced from print resolution (300+ DPI) to screen resolution (100–150 DPI). On statements and contracts, you cannot see the difference, because these pages contain text and simple graphics, not photography.

Step 2 — Re-encode images. Scanned pages get recompressed with JPEG-style encoding tuned for document content, which is far more efficient than the raw capture format.

Step 3 — Clean overhead. Duplicate fonts, embedded previews, metadata, and save-history layers are stripped out. Multi-page PDFs from scanners accumulate surprising amounts of this invisible weight.

Step 4 — Iterate to target. The tool compresses, measures, and adjusts in small steps until the file lands under 1 MB, stopping at the highest quality that fits.

What I like about this approach is that text-heavy documents — statements, contracts, tax forms — compress beautifully at 1 MB. The numbers stay crisp because text is vector data, and only the background scans get squeezed.
