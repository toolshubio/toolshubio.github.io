Keyword Frequency Counter — See Exactly How Often Each Word Appears

I once edited a 2,000-word article where the writer had used the word "leverage" 31 times. Nobody noticed while writing — repetition is invisible to the author and glaring to everyone else. A keyword frequency counter makes the invisible visible: paste any text and get every word ranked by how often it appears, with counts and percentages. It is part SEO tool, part writing mirror.

What Is a Keyword Frequency Counter?

At its core, it counts: each unique word in your text, how many times it occurs, and what share of the total that represents. The output is a ranked table — most frequent first — that instantly shows which terms dominate your writing. SEO folks read it as a keyword distribution check; writers read it as a repetition audit; linguists read it as raw corpus data.

Frequency and density are siblings, not twins. Frequency is the raw count ("coffee" appears 12 times); density is that count as a percentage of total words (12 in 600 words = 2%). Counters typically show both, because the count tells you what to edit and the percentage tells you whether it matters.

The stop-word toggle is what makes the table useful. Without it, "the," "and," and "to" top every list and bury the interesting words. Filtering those function words surfaces the topical vocabulary — the nouns and verbs your piece is actually about — which is what you came to inspect.

How to Use the Keyword Frequency Counter

Paste your text into the analyzer — a draft, a published article, a competitor's page. Enable "ignore common words" and case-insensitive counting (so "Word" and "word" merge). Scan the top 20–50 rows: your target topics should rank highly, but no single term should tower absurdly over the rest.

Two passes cover most use cases. Pass one: stuffing check. If your focus keyword sits at 4%+ while everything else is under 1%, rewrite the worst repetitions with pronouns and synonyms. Pass two: gap check. Paste a top-ranking competitor's text and compare tables — topical terms they use heavily that barely appear in yours are coverage gaps worth addressing in your own words.

Writers get a bonus use: sort by frequency to find your crutch words. Everyone has them — "actually," "basically," "leverage," "robust." The counter finds yours in seconds, and a quick find-and-replace pass tightens the whole piece.

How Word Frequency Analysis Works

The pipeline is classic text analysis, fast enough to run as you type.

Step 1 — Tokenization. Text is split into word tokens using Unicode-aware boundaries — punctuation separated, accented characters and non-Latin scripts handled correctly, words lowercased for consistent counting.

Step 2 — Stop-word filtering. Optionally, common function words (the, a, and, is, of) are excluded so the table highlights meaningful terms. Minimum word length filters can further trim noise.

Step 3 — Counting and ranking. Each unique word's occurrences are tallied, percentages computed against the total, and the table sorted most-frequent first. Many tools also offer bigram/trigram modes for phrase-level analysis.

Step 4 — Presentation. Results render as a sortable table with counts, percentages, and often visual bars — plus export options (CSV) for deeper analysis in spreadsheets.

Because analysis runs locally in the browser, even unpublished drafts and confidential documents stay private — nothing uploads anywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this keyword frequency counter free?

Yes — free with no account required.

What's the difference between frequency and density?

Frequency is the raw count of a word's occurrences; density is that count as a percentage of total words. The table shows both.

How is this different from a keyword density checker?

They overlap heavily. Frequency counters emphasize the full ranked word list (useful for writing audits); density checkers emphasize percentages against thresholds (useful for SEO checks). Many people use both views of the same data.

Should "the" and "and" top my list?

In raw counts, yes — they are the most common English words. Enable stop-word filtering to see your topical vocabulary instead.

Can it handle large documents?

Yes. Most tools process thousands of words instantly, and many handle full book chapters without breaking a sweat.

Does it work in languages other than English?

Unicode-aware counters handle accented characters and many scripts. Stop-word lists are usually English-focused, so filtering quality varies by language.

Is my text private?

Yes. Analysis runs entirely in your browser — your text is never uploaded to any server.
