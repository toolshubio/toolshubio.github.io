What Is TIFF?

TIFF (Tagged Image File Format) dates back to the 1980s — Aldus Corporation created it, Adobe owns it now — and it remains the standard for professional photography pipelines, print prepress, and scanners. It supports lossless compression (LZW, ZIP/Deflate, PackBits) or no compression at all, high bit depths up to 32-bit, CMYK color, transparency, and even multiple pages in one file (scanned documents love this).

The price of all that capability is size. A 24-megapixel TIFF runs 36–144 MB; a 600 DPI letter-size scan can hit 25–100 MB. And browsers cannot display TIFF at all — no native rendering in Chrome, Firefox, or Safari. It is a format built for editing and archiving, not for sharing.

Why Convert TIFF to JPG?

  • Email-able scans — a quality-92 JPG of that 600 DPI scan lands at 1–4 MB, comfortably under Gmail's and Outlook's 25 MB caps.
  • Universal compatibility — JPG opens on every device, browser, email client, and social platform in existence.
  • Web publishing — browsers cannot display TIFF; JPG is the web's native photo format.
  • Social sharing — Instagram, Facebook, and X accept JPG and reject TIFF.
  • Cheaper storage — converting an archive to JPG cuts storage needs by 90–95% where lossless is not required.

The professional workflow this implies: edit and archive in TIFF, export to JPG for delivery. The conversion is the last step, not the storage format.

How to Convert TIFF to JPG

Step 1 — Upload your TIFF/TIF files. Drag and drop or click to browse. Batch upload handles whole archives; multi-page TIFFs are accepted.

Step 2 — Choose quality and size. Quality in the low 80s is the sweet spot for sharing — visually indistinguishable from the TIFF for photographic content at a fraction of the size. Low 90s for client delivery or print. Optionally scale down the resolution; a 600 DPI scan rarely needs full resolution for screen viewing.

Step 3 — Convert and download. Each page of a multi-page TIFF typically becomes its own JPG, numbered sequentially, with a ZIP offered for multi-file results.

As a rough guide to the savings: at quality 85, an 82 MB TIFF becomes roughly a 7.6 MB JPG (about 91% smaller); at quality 95, closer to 18 MB; at quality 70, around 3.5 MB. The file shrinks dramatically long before your eyes notice.

Quality Considerations

16-bit color gets downsampled. TIFF may hold 16-bit color data; JPG is 8-bit per channel. For color-critical work, handle tone mapping in Lightroom or Photoshop rather than a quick converter.

CMYK TIFFs need care. Print-oriented TIFFs in CMYK color may shift when converted to JPG's RGB. Check the colors in the preview.

Never convert your only copy. JPG compression is lossy and cumulative. Archive the TIFF; convert copies for sharing.

Scans compress beautifully. Document scans are mostly white space and text — JPG handles them efficiently, which is why a 50 MB scan becomes a 2 MB email-friendly file.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this TIFF to JPG converter free?

Yes — free, no account, no watermarks.

Will converting reduce quality?

At quality 85+, the difference is imperceptible for photographic content. For critical color work, keep the TIFF.

Can I convert multi-page TIFFs?

Yes — each page becomes its own JPG, usually numbered sequentially and offered as a ZIP.

Why is my TIFF so large?

TIFF stores data losslessly (or uncompressed) at high bit depths. A 24 MP photo is 36–144 MB; the same image as JPG is 4–10 MB.

Can I convert multiple TIFFs at once?

Yes — batch conversion is supported.

What quality setting should I use?

Low 80s for web and sharing, low 90s for client delivery and print.

Are my files uploaded to a server?

Browser-based converters process locally; server-based ones should auto-delete files after processing.

Should I delete the TIFF after converting?

No — archive it. The TIFF is your lossless master; the JPG is the shareable working copy.

What is a TIFF file?
