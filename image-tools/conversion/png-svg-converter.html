PNG is my favorite format for one very specific job: it is the best raster format for graphics with transparency. But it is still raster — a fixed grid of pixels. I learned this the painful way when a startup's beautiful 512-pixel PNG logo had to go on the side of a van, and the print shop asked for a vector file. A PNG to SVG converter redraws your pixel graphic as true vector paths, and suddenly that logo works at any size, from a browser tab to a billboard.

Why Convert PNG to SVG?

PNG shines for web graphics: lossless quality, full transparency, universal support. Its weakness is scale. A PNG rendered at 200 pixels looks crisp at 200 pixels and soft everywhere else. Converting to SVG solves the cases where pixels run out:

  • Logos that must scale — one SVG replaces a whole folder of PNGs at different resolutions.
  • Icons and UI assets — crisp on every screen density, including future ones.
  • Cut files — vinyl cutters, laser engravers, and embroidery machines work from vector paths, not pixels.
  • Design workflows — Figma, Illustrator, and Inkscape edit vectors natively.
  • Tiny file sizes — a simple traced logo is often a few kilobytes, far smaller than its PNG.

How PNG to SVG Tracing Works

A proper converter does not just rename the file — it traces it. The process, used by tools like ImageTracer.js in the browser and Inkscape's Trace Bitmap on the desktop, runs roughly like this:

Step 1 — Color quantization. The image's colors are reduced to a palette — say 8 or 16 colors. Fewer colors mean cleaner, simpler vector shapes.

Step 2 — Edge detection and path fitting. The tracer finds boundaries between color regions and fits smooth Bézier curves along them, turning pixel stair-steps into clean geometry.

Step 3 — Path output. The curves are written as SVG path data — human-readable XML you can open, edit, and recolor in any vector editor.

A detail setting (low/medium/high) trades file size against fidelity: more paths capture more detail but bloat the file. Transparency in the PNG is typically preserved as transparent regions in the SVG — one advantage PNG sources have over JPG.

The rule is simple: the flatter and cleaner the graphic, the better the trace. A two-color logo is a thirty-second job; a product photo is a lost cause. And garbage in means garbage out — a blurry, artifact-ridden PNG traces its own flaws faithfully, so start from the cleanest source you have.

How to Convert PNG to SVG

Step 1 — Upload your PNG. Drag and drop or click to browse. Use the highest-resolution version available.

Step 2 — Tune the trace. Set the color count (fewer for logos, more for illustrations), detail level, and smoothing. Preview the result — zoom in and check that curves are smooth and colors are right.

Step 3 — Download and verify. Open the SVG in a browser or vector editor at several sizes. A good trace looks identical to the PNG at every zoom level.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this PNG to SVG converter free?

Yes — free, no account, no watermark.

Will my PNG become a true vector?

Yes, with a tracing converter — it redraws your graphic as vector paths. Beware of tools that merely embed the PNG inside an SVG wrapper; those do not scale any better.

What images trace best?

Logos, icons, and flat graphics with clean edges and few colors. Photos and gradients trace poorly.

Is transparency preserved?

Yes — transparent regions of the PNG become transparent areas in the SVG.

Why does my traced logo have jagged edges?

Usually a low-resolution or noisy source. Trace from the largest, cleanest PNG you have, and increase the smoothing setting.

Will the SVG be smaller than the PNG?

For simple graphics, dramatically smaller. For complex images, the SVG can be larger — another reason to trace only suitable artwork.

Can I edit the SVG afterward?

Yes — open it in Inkscape, Illustrator, Figma, or a text editor to adjust shapes, colors, and paths.

Are my files uploaded to a server?

No — browser-based tracing processes everything locally on your device.

Frequently asked

Which PNGs vectorize well?
Logos, icons, illustrations and line art with clear shapes and limited colors. Photos and gradients produce huge, inaccurate files.
Will the SVG look exactly like my PNG?
Close for simple graphics, approximate for complex ones. Use the detail and simplify controls to dial in the balance.
Why is my SVG file so large?
Too much detail or too many colors. Lower the color count and raise simplification to shrink it.
Are my files uploaded?
No — tracing happens entirely in your browser.
