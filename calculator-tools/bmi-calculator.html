I first calculated my BMI at a gym induction, where the trainer scribbled weight ÷ height² on a whiteboard and circled the result. What Is a BMI Calculator?

BMI (Body Mass Index) is a single number that relates your weight to your height. The formula is simple: BMI = weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. A person who is 70 kg and 1.75 m tall has a BMI of 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 22.9 — squarely in the healthy range.

The result is interpreted against standard categories used worldwide: below 18.5 is underweight, 18.5–24.9 is healthy weight, 25–29.9 is overweight, and 30 or above is obese. These cutoffs come from large population studies linking BMI ranges to health risk, which is why doctors, insurers, and fitness programs all speak the same BMI language.

One honest caveat I always mention: BMI does not distinguish muscle from fat. Athletes with heavy muscle mass can score "overweight" while being very fit, and it is less reliable for children, pregnant women, and the elderly. It is a screening number, not a diagnosis — useful, but not the whole story.

How to Use the BMI Calculator

Enter your height and weight, choosing metric (cm/kg) or imperial (feet-inches/pounds) units — whichever you actually measured in. Click Calculate and your BMI appears instantly, usually with your category and the healthy weight range for your height.

The unit choice is where mistakes happen. Entering 175 cm where meters are expected, or pounds where kilograms are expected, produces a wildly wrong number. A good calculator labels its fields clearly; still, glance at the result before trusting it — a BMI of 0.02 or 700 means the units got mixed up.

One habit worth building: measure in the morning, on the same scale, wearing similar clothing. Weight fluctuates through the day, and consistent conditions make month-to-month comparisons meaningful instead of noisy.

How BMI Is Calculated

One formula, two unit systems — and the calculator handles both.

Metric formula. BMI = weight (kg) ÷ height (m)². Example: 68 kg, 165 cm → convert to 1.65 m → 1.65² = 2.7225 → 68 ÷ 2.7225 = 24.98.

Imperial formula. BMI = (weight (lb) ÷ height (in)²) × 703. Example: 150 lb, 5'5" (65 in) → 150 ÷ 4225 × 703 = 24.96. The 703 is just the conversion factor that puts imperial measurements on the same scale as metric.

Category lookup. The result is matched to the standard table: <18.5 underweight, 18.5–24.9 healthy, 25–29.9 overweight, ≥30 obese (with classes I, II, III at 30, 35, 40). The calculator also commonly shows your healthy weight range — the weights that would put you between 18.5 and 24.9 at your height.

Worked example — 85 kg at 1.80 m: 1.80² = 3.24 → 85 ÷ 3.24 = 26.2 → overweight category. The arithmetic is trivial; remembering to square the height in meters (not centimeters) is the part people get wrong by hand.

Who Uses BMI Calculators?

Anyone starting a fitness plan uses BMI as a baseline number to track over months, alongside measurements that BMI misses like waist size.

Doctors and clinics use it as a quick screening step during checkups, flagging patients for deeper assessment.

Insurance and corporate wellness programs use BMI brackets for risk assessment and health incentives.

Parents check children's growth against age-adjusted BMI percentiles (a different interpretation from the adult table).

Students in health and nutrition courses practice the formula and its interpretation with instant feedback.

Frequently asked

What is a healthy BMI?

18.5–24.9 is the standard healthy range, though athletes with high muscle mass can read higher without extra health risk.

How accurate is the US Navy body-fat method?

It is a solid estimate from tape measurements — close enough for tracking trends, though not as precise as a DEXA scan.

Is BMI accurate for athletes?

Not always. Muscle weighs more than fat, so very muscular people should rely more on the body-fat estimate.

Is my data stored?

No — all calculations run locally in your browser.
