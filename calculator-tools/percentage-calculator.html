I once watched a friend argue with a cashier for five minutes about whether "30% off, then an extra 20% off" meant half price. (It doesn't — it's 44% off, and the cashier was right.) Percentages show up in discounts, tips, exam scores, tax bills, and profit margins, and they are just awkward enough to do in your head that most people either guess or pull out their phone. A percentage calculator removes the guessing: pick the kind of calculation, enter two numbers, and read the answer with the formula shown so you can trust it.

What a Percentage Calculator Does

Despite the simple name, there are really five different calculations hiding under "percentages," and mixing them up is where most errors happen:

X% of Y — the classic. "What is 15% of 200?" Answer: 30. Use it for discounts, tips, and tax amounts.

X is what % of Y — the reverse. "45 is what percent of 200?" Answer: 22.5%. Use it for exam scores (450 ÷ 500 × 100 = 90%), grade checks, and budget shares.

Increase/decrease by a percentage — "Increase 150 by 20%" gives 180; "decrease 250 by 10%" gives 225. Shopping sale prices and salary raises live here.

Percentage change — "My bill went from $150 to $180; what is the increase?" Answer: 20%. The tool handles increases and decreases and shows the direction.

Reverse percentage — "I paid $1,350 after a 10% discount; what was the original price?" Since $1,350 is 90% of the original, the answer is $1,500. Handy for back-calculating before-tax or pre-discount prices.

This calculator covers all five in a standard mode, then goes further with dedicated modes for batch work, profit analysis, and tax — which is why it earns a permanent spot in people's bookmarks.

The Formulas Behind Each Mode

You never need to memorize these — the tool shows the matching formula note with every result — but seeing them once makes the modes click:

CalculationFormulaExample
X% of Y(X ÷ 100) × Y15% of 200 = 30
X is what % of Y(X ÷ Y) × 10045 is 22.5% of 200
Increase Y by X%Y × (1 + X ÷ 100)150 + 20% = 180
Decrease Y by X%Y × (1 − X ÷ 100)250 − 10% = 225
Percentage change((New − Old) ÷ Old) × 100150 → 180 = +20%
Reverse percentagePaid ÷ (1 − Discount ÷ 100)$1,350 at 10% off → $1,500

The one people get wrong most often is stacked discounts. "30% off then 20% off" is not 50% off: the second discount applies to the already-reduced price, so 100 × 0.70 × 0.80 = 56, meaning you pay 56% and save 44%. Run it through the increase/decrease mode twice and you will never argue with a cashier again.

How to Use the Percentage Calculator

Pick the mode first — the two input fields change meaning depending on which calculation you choose.

Step 1 — Choose the calculation type. Standard mode covers the five calculations above. Batch mode handles lists of values at once. Profit & Loss mode is for margins and markups. Tax mode handles VAT/GST.

Step 2 — Enter the two values. For "X% of Y," enter the percentage first and the base amount second. For percentage change, enter the old value first and the new value second — order matters here. The labels update per mode so you always know which box is which.

Step 4 — Export when needed. Batch results can be exported to CSV or PDF — useful when you are checking a column of invoice figures or sharing discount math with a team.

Profit Margin vs Markup (Profit & Loss Mode)

This mode exists because margin and markup are the most confused pair in small-business math. Markup is profit as a percentage of cost: buy at $80, sell at $100, and the markup is ($100 − $80) ÷ $80 = 25%. Margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price: the same $20 profit is $20 ÷ $100 = 20% margin.

The distinction matters because a 25% markup is not a 25% margin — using the wrong one silently misprices your products. Enter cost and selling price (or either one plus the target percentage) and the calculator returns both figures side by side, so you price with the number you actually mean.

Batch Processing and Tax Mode

Batch mode applies one percentage to a whole list of values — paste a column of prices, enter the discount once, and get every result in a table. Retail staff repricing inventory and accountants checking invoice lines use this constantly; doing fifty values one at a time is exactly the kind of tedium the tool exists to kill.

Tax mode handles VAT and GST both ways: add tax to a net amount or strip tax out of a gross amount. A $100 item with 18% GST costs $118 gross; working backward, $118 gross at 18% GST means a net of $100. E-commerce sellers and freelancers quoting clients in tax-inclusive markets use this daily.

Frequently asked

How do I find X% of Y?

Multiply Y by X/100 — the Standard mode does it instantly.

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin is profit as a share of selling price; markup is profit as a share of cost.

How is percent change calculated?

(New − Old) / Old × 100 — positive for increases, negative for decreases.

Can I process many values at once?

Yes — Batch mode applies one percentage to a whole list.
